How the scoring works

Two numbers on a cat-food bag mislead people managing a sensitive gut: the crude protein percentage (which counts plant protein equally with meat) and the “gastrointestinal” or “grain-free” positioning (which often means added fermentable fiber). This index re-scores every food on what actually matters for a carnivore with bacterial overgrowth.

Axis 1 — FODMAP rating (prebiotic-gated)

The rating hinges on one distinction: purified prebiotics (fructooligosaccharides/FOS, inulin, chicory root) versus whole legumes (peas, lentils, chickpeas). Purified prebiotics are added specifically to ferment — they are potent substrate for a small-intestinal overgrowth even in tiny amounts. Whole legumes are a milder, slower-fermenting concern. So the scale gates on the former:

Excellent
Nothing fermentable. No prebiotics, no legumes.
Good
Only trace fermentable content (e.g. pea starch, a yeast-derived MOS).
Moderate
Whole legumes present, but no purified prebiotic. Considerable — watch stool.
Poor
Contains a purified prebiotic (FOS/inulin/chicory), or a heavy legume load.
Avoid
Purified prebiotic plus a heavy fermentable load.
The guiding principle: if a food is even a candidate for a sensitive gut, it isn’t a true “Poor.” Whole legumes alone never push past Moderate; it takes an added purified prebiotic to sink a food.

Axis 2 — Animal-protein index (0–100)

An estimate of what fraction of a food’s protein comes from animal sources rather than plant concentrates (pea protein, corn gluten meal, soy protein isolate, potato protein). It is a position-weighted ingredient heuristic, not a lab assay — ingredients earlier in the deck count more. It exists because crude protein % hides the difference between a 50%-protein food built on soy isolate and one built on chicken.

Known limitation: the index credits any animal-derived ingredient, so hydrolyzed or feather/by-product proteins score as “animal” despite poor biological quality. A hydrolyzed-liver or feather-meal elimination diet can read High on this axis while being a poor muscle food. Read the ingredient deck, not just the band.

Why these two axes together

A food has to clear both to suit a carnivore with overgrowth: clean FODMAP (won’t feed the bacteria) and high animal protein (rebuilds muscle, is absorbed high in the gut rather than fermenting lower down). Most of the market fails one or the other. The premium grain-free shelf tends to fail FODMAP (legumes + chicory/inulin). The prescription shelf tends to fail animal protein (corn gluten, soy isolate) and often FODMAP too (added FOS).

About this index

This began as one household’s attempt to feed a Siamese with antibiotic-responsive enteropathy — a cat whose flares behave like a self-reinforcing loop of small-intestinal overgrowth and transient pancreatitis. The commercial market, it turned out, offers almost nothing that is simultaneously low-calorie, high in animal protein, and free of fermentable prebiotics. Proving that took scoring the whole shelf. This is that scoring, generalized.

What it is

A reference for the direction of a food — a fast way to see, across hundreds of products, which ones are animal-forward and free of the fermentable additives that trouble an overgrown gut, and which ones only look that way on the front of the bag.

What it is not

It is not veterinary advice, and it is not a complete-and-balanced judgment. Guaranteed-analysis figures are label minimums, not measured values. The animal index is a ranking heuristic, not an assay. A food’s suitability for your cat depends on their specific diagnosis, and on things no database can see — palatability, stool response, whether a food even dispenses through your feeder. Use this to narrow a shelf, then confirm with your own cat and your own vet.

Not veterinary advice. Always confirm dietary changes with your veterinarian, especially for a cat with a diagnosed condition. Ingredient decks change without notice — verify the current label before buying.

Sourcing

Scores are computed from published ingredient decks and guaranteed-analysis panels. Ratings are derived by a fixed rubric (see Method), not entered by hand, so they’re consistent across foods. Where a deck was later verified against the manufacturer’s full label, the verdict was re-confirmed.
